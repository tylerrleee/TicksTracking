## Project Overview

Ticks are expanding their range in the U.S., carrying diseases such as Lyme disease and Rocky Mountain spotted fever. This project investigates how three climatic variables—temperature, relative humidity, and precipitation—affect tick population dynamics (nymph and adult stages) across three NEON sites (HARV, UNDE, ORNL) from 2014 to 2024.

## Background & Motivation

- **Why it matters:** Tick‑borne illnesses pose growing public health threats as climate change alters environmental conditions.  
- **Hypothesis:** Rising temperatures, elevated humidity, and shifting precipitation patterns create more favorable conditions for tick survival, reproduction, and geographic expansion, particularly in the eastern U.S.

## Data Sources

- **NEON Tick Data** (2014–2024)  
- **NEON Climate Observations** (temperature, humidity, precipitation)  
- **Site Locations:**  
  - Harvard Forest (HARV), MA  
  - University of Notre Dame Environmental Research Center (UNDE), WI/MI  
  - Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), TN

## Key Findings

- **Temperature**: Optimal tick activity between 20–25 °C; ORNL showed stronger temperature–tick correlations than HARV.
- **Humidity**: Peak tick counts at 70–80 % RH; HARV ticks preferred slightly lower RH (70–75 %) vs. ORNL (75–80 %).
- **Precipitation**: No clear linear relationship; both sites experienced predominantly light rainfall (< 0.6 mm), limiting statistical power—literature suggests moderate moisture benefits ticks but heavy rains inhibit activity.
